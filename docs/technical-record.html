Nila

An offline infant-care companion for Android: it listens for a cry, tries to settle the baby before it wakes anyone, and answers questions about the baby and the breastfeeding mother without a single network request.

01What it is

Every baby monitor on the market ends at notify. That treats a baby who would have self-settled in forty seconds identically to one who needs a parent, and it is why monitors wake households that did not need waking.

Nila adds two rungs before that. When a cry passes twenty seconds it plays a sound — the caregiver's own recorded voice, if there is one. Thirty-five seconds later it re-reads the loudness envelope and works out whether the sound actually helped. Only then does it wake somebody, and the alert says what it tried first.

That verification step is the product. The system does not claim the sound worked; it measures whether it did, remembers the answer per sound, and lets that change what it plays next time.

Around that sit four more things a parent of a newborn actually needs at 3am, all of them local: a searchable shelf of health documents for both the baby and the mother, an assistant that answers from a curated corpus rather than from a model's imagination, a medicine-safety check for a breastfeeding mother built as a staged review, and objective cry-duration tracking against the colic criteria so a paediatrician gets a real count instead of an exhausted estimate.

02The central finding

Cry detection works. Cry cause does not — and the number that separates them is the most important thing in this project.

Almost every published infant-cry classifier reports 85–95% accuracy on the Donate-a-Cry corpus. Those numbers come from splitting clips at random. One infant contributes several clips, so the same baby appears in both train and test, and the model learns to recognise the infant rather than the reason. Split by infant instead — no baby in both halves — and the task collapses.

Leakage alone is worth +0.51 AUC on the identical model and the identical data. The legacy Random Forest in the Why-is-my-Baby-Crying repository scores 0.990 on our held-out infants only because it was fitted on all 457 clips, those infants included. Retrained on the same 461 features with an honest split it lands at 0.462, with balanced accuracy of exactly 0.200 — it predicts hungry for everything, which is the majority class.

Two unrelated model families failing identically is not a tuning problem. Four label groupings were also tried — five-way, hungry-vs-rest, pain-vs-rest, three-way — and every one sat at chance.

What the app does about it

The cause estimate still runs and is still shown, because hiding it would be its own kind of dishonesty. It appears under the heading UNVALIDATED GUESS, carrying its own confidence and, when the top two classes are within 15 points, the runner-up as well. The trustworthy flag is wired into the Kotlin type rather than left to the UI to remember, and ModelCard.kt is generated from the evaluation reports so no claim on screen can drift from a measurement.

Cry detection

working

The RandomForest from the original project, retrained by its own pipeline and exported whole — 500 trees walked on the phone in about a millisecond. Shown as "Probably X", with a plain note that it is a guess from the sound. Section 02 has the numbers.

Three of five windows above 0.62 to enter, six consecutive below 0.42 to exit. Duration, peak loudness, rising/steady/settling trend and a 1 Hz envelope, all measured. Duration counts the audio clock, not the wall clock.

Live microphone meter

working

New. A dBFS bar and cry score that move while listening, plus an explicit warning after a minute of complete silence. Added because "Listening" alone is indistinguishable from a dead microphone.

Demo cry replay

working

Feeds a real recorded cry through the real pipeline in real time — same frontend, same models, same ladder, same notification. Both soothing sounds play aloud at the second the ladder reaches them, so the clip coming out of the speaker and the timer on screen agree; the summary is held on screen until it is dismissed. About three minutes, the length of the cry. Labelled on screen so it can never be mistaken for the microphone.

The cry itself now comes out of the speaker, ducked under whichever sound is being tried, so the middle rung has something audible to be soothing and the verification rung has something audible to have judged. The sound playing is drawn as a voice note with a moving waveform rather than a line of text, because a status line that changes and changes back reads as a log entry and not as an action the phone is taking — the most common reaction to the old demo was not noticing the soothing had happened.

What is scripted, and why

Two things, and both are worth naming.

The clip is an excerpt. The source recording is six seconds, of which only part is cry-like to the detector: scored window by window, 36 of 61 fall below the 0.42 exit threshold, with low runs of five against a hysteresis exit of six. The episode dropped out and restarted mid-demo. A search over every sub-segment found a 1.5 s stretch that loops with a longest low run of two and a mean of 0.70 — it enters the episode in two seconds and holds it. That excerpt, crossfaded at the seam and tiled to six seconds, is the shipped asset.

The loudness is supplied. Looping anything makes its envelope repeat, and the trend estimator — a least-squares slope over the last twenty windows — reads that repetition as rising or settling depending on nothing more than where the loop wrapped. The ladder escalated at an arbitrary point, so the demo told a different story every run. Each window is now normalised onto a fixed arc: a rise into the first attempt, then a plateau long enough for both attempts to be played and judged. A flat plateau draws a dead sparkline, so it carries a two-component ripple — 4 dB at eight windows against 2.5 dB at six — chosen by measuring the residual slope across every phase and offset. It peaks at 0.051 dB against the estimator's 0.12 dB threshold, and four JVM tests hold it there.

Audio, frontend, detector, classifier, hysteresis and every rung of the ladder are untouched. How loud a simulated baby is happens to be the one quantity a recording of a different baby cannot supply.

The cause estimate is also latched for the episode. It runs on every window and, being at chance on infants it has never heard, returns a different label on many of them: one run announced tired in the transcript, showed discomfort on the card and woke the parent with belly pain. Three answers to one question reads as a broken app rather than an honest one. It is decided once from the cry and then stands — still as "probably", still never as a fact.

The demo family also ships one sample caregiver recording, so a fresh install can demonstrate what "it plays your own voice first" sounds like. It is a synthesised stand-in, not anyone's real voice, and it is written into the recordings directory the same way one made in the app would be — so the monitor finds it through the ordinary path. Recording over it in Settings replaces it, which is what anyone demonstrating this should do.

04How cry detection works

One frontend, two heads, hysteresis in between.

The frontend

Audio is captured at 16 kHz mono from AudioSource.UNPROCESSED where the device offers it. The usual VOICE_RECOGNITION source applies noise suppression and automatic gain control tuned for speech, and AGC in particular flattens the loudness envelope the escalation logic reads — so it is avoided.

Every 480 ms a window covering the last 0.96 s is emitted, so consecutive windows overlap by half. The overlap matters: a cry that starts mid-window would otherwise be split across two patches and score weakly in both, which is exactly the onset the detector most needs to catch.

Each window becomes a 96 × 64 log-mel patch — 25 ms frames, 10 ms hop, 64 mel bands from 125 Hz to 7500 Hz. These are YAMNet's parameters, chosen so the representation is a known quantity rather than an invention.

A frontend written twice

The log-mel transform exists in Python for training and in Kotlin for inference, and they must agree to the last bit or the model sees a different distribution on device than it was trained on. A golden-fixture test pins them together.

Chasing a 0.0022 discrepancy cost real time. The first suspect was float32 twiddle-factor accumulation in the Kotlin FFT, which was duly fixed with double precision and a precomputed table. The actual cause was the fixture generator rounding its inputs to seven decimals, while quiet frames contain samples around 1e−6.

The detector

A small convolutional network — four separable blocks, 21,218 parameters, 38 KB after dynamic-range quantisation to TFLite. Delegates are attempted in order NNAPI → GPU → CPU, each with a probe inference before it is trusted, because MediaPipe's GPU delegate will construct successfully and then throw GL_INVALID_ENUM on the first real frame.

Hard-negative mining

The first detector fired on cats in five of eight test clips. Scoring every training negative with the trained model and oversampling the hardest 18% — a set dominated by cat, laughing, sheep, rooster, crow and toilet flush — took that to zero of eight, and lifted cry F1 from 0.922 to 0.942.

Training data

Two corpora. ESC-50 and UrbanSound8K supply negatives — 50 environmental classes including the ones that actually fool a cry detector. Donate-a-Cry supplies 457 labelled cry clips from 221 infants, whose distribution is the problem: 382 of the 457 are hungry, and burping has 8 clips across 7 infants.

How the split is made

Each subject is assigned to exactly one side by greedy iterative stratification, globally, before anything else happens. An earlier version split per class and an assertion caught four infant UUIDs on both sides — one baby can contribute clips under several labels, so per-class splitting leaks subjects even when each class looks clean.

Augmentation runs strictly after the split. Augmenting first would put time-stretched copies of a training clip into the test set, which is leakage wearing a different hat. A --leaky flag reproduces the opposite ordering deliberately, as the comparison arm in section 02.

The cause classifier is the original project's

The five-way classifier is no longer a CNN trained here. It is the RandomForest from Why-is-my-Baby-Crying, retrained by that project's own pipeline — its augmentation (time-stretch, pitch-shift, noise, gain, circular shift, two picked at random per copy), its SMOTE balancing, its 500 trees at depth 20 with balanced class weights — and exported whole rather than converted. The phone walks the trees, so predict_proba on the phone is predict_proba in Python.

One change was needed to make it fit. The original computes 466 librosa features including chroma and tonnetz; those two contribute almost nothing (tonnetz is 0.004 of the top-40 importance, chroma does not appear) and are the most awkward to reimplement. The 456 features that ship are derived from the log-mel frontend that already has a bit-checked Kotlin twin, so the whole vector inherits that guarantee instead of opening a second chance for the two languages to disagree.

06Escalation & actions

The rungs are timed, each one is a decision the system can explain, and every one of them is now written to the screen as it happens.

  1. 0–12 s — nothing. A fuss that resolves itself is not an event. Logged silently.
  2. 12 s — note it. One entry in the log, no sound, no alert.
  3. 20 s — play something. The caregiver's recorded voice if one exists, otherwise the built-in sound with the best measured record for this baby, with a 15% chance of exploring an alternative. Skipped entirely if a call or the parent's own music holds audio focus. If an infrared code has been taught, it fires here too, once.
  4. 55 s — verify. Re-read the envelope. Settled means a settling trend or a drop of 18 dB from peak. The outcome is written to that sound's record whether it worked or not.
  5. 55 s, still rising — stop trying. A cry that is building well after an attempt is not going to settle. Escalate.
  6. 100 s — hard ceiling. Wake a parent regardless of trend. The notification names what was tried: "Crying for 58s and getting louder. Tried 2 sounds first."

The soother cannot feed itself

The sound plays through the same phone's speaker, a metre from the same phone's microphone, so it has to be checked: if a soother scored as a cry, the ladder would hear its own output, treat the episode as continuing, and escalate on its own noise.

Measured through the real detector: white noise 0.198, heartbeat 0.088, shushing 0.007. The episode entry threshold is 0.62 and, more importantly, the exit threshold is 0.42 — all three sit well under both, so a running episode still ends normally while a soother plays. A test pins this, because a future soother chosen for how it sounds could easily break it.

Alerts carry distinct vibration patterns per severity so a wrist is readable without looking — which is the point of routing them to a watch, since the phone stays silent next to a sleeping baby. Safety alerts use a channel that bypasses Do Not Disturb; nothing else in the app does.

07The local language model

Retrieval, not fine-tuning — and now, not generation either

It is a RAG system, and the model is not fine-tuned at all. Answers are BM25 retrieval over a hand-curated corpus, shown verbatim with their source.

The 0.5B model used to rewrite those answers into nicer prose. It no longer does — in any health-facing path. Why is below, and it is the single most important thing on this page after the cry-cause result.

What actually answers the question

  1. Route. Before any retrieval, QueryRouter decides whether this is an emergency, a greeting, too vague, a dose request, a diagnosis request, out of scope, or answerable — and whether the subject is the baby or the mother.
  2. Retrieve. BM25 over 47 documents, filtered by audience and by the baby's age in months, so newborn guidance is never returned for a nine-month-old.
  3. Answer. The retrieved text is composed into an answer with its sources and shown immediately. This is the answer.
  4. Show her own records, underneath. Matching documents from the health shelf are appended below the answer under their own heading — never above it. Anything quoted at the top of an answer reads as being the answer, and "what should I eat while breastfeeding" was opening with a prescription for iron and an inhaler, which mentions breastfeeding and answers nothing.

Why nothing is generated any more

Asked "I don't feel bonded with my baby", the model returned, among other things:

"The baby has not yet passed the newborn hearing screening, which is a sign of potential hearing issues."

No such thing had happened. It had taken a health record stating the screening was passed, inverted it, and attached a diagnosis — for a real baby, to a mother already asking about her own bonding.

The grounding check did not catch it, and no version of that check can. It measures how much of the output's vocabulary comes from the source, and every word of that sentence did. Word overlap cannot see a flipped negation or a clause reassembled into a claim the source never made.

So generation is gone from the answer path, as it was already gone from the medicine path, and for the same reason. The retrieved answer was always complete, sourced and readable — the version that replaced it was fluent and wrong. An instrumented test now asserts that every guidance answer appears verbatim in the corpus.

The model

Removed, then brought back on different terms

Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct runs on device through MediaPipe's LlmInference. It was taken out entirely after the hearing-screening incident, and it is back — doing a narrower job, under conditions chosen so that failure cannot repeat.

The structural half is that it is never shown a health record. The sentence that removed the feature was assembled out of one; a model that cannot see them cannot invert one, and that is a stronger guarantee than any instruction in a prompt.

The check half is polarity. The old grounding test measured how much of the output's vocabulary came from the source and passed that sentence at 100%, because every word of it did. Each output sentence is now matched to the source sentence it most resembles, and the two have to agree about negation. Why generation is banned from the medicine path

It was wired there and removed after watching it produce this, from a correct AVOID verdict for a penicillin-allergic mother:

"Amoxicillin … could cause asthma in some people and iron deficiency anaemia in others … it was generally safe to take Amoxicillin if you are not allergic to it."

It had read her conditions as side effects, invented causation, and then softened a do-not-take. A 0.5B model rewriting safety-critical text is not a quality trade-off, it is a hazard — and the rule-based summary it was "improving" was already correct, sourced and readable.

08How good is it, measured

Three batteries of real questions, written the way people type at 2am. The number that counts is the third one.

"Improve the assistant" is not actionable until you can say how good it is now. So the questions were written first, run through the real router and the real retrieval, and scored against the document that should have answered each one. Everything after that was driven by the failures.

Each battery scores 100% eventually because each one was used to find bugs. That is what an evaluation set is for, and it also destroys it as a measurement — after you have looked at the failures, the set is training data.

The honest figure is 25/41 on first contact with the third battery, against 54/77 on the first. The improvement between those two is real; the 93% that followed is a regression guard, not a claim.

What was actually wrong

  1. No stemming. "Dehydrated" and "dehydration" were unrelated terms, so the question returned nothing at all — which the app honestly reported as knowing nothing about dehydration. A five-rule suffix stripper, plus synonyms for the irregulars ("slept", "fed", "woke") and the words people use instead ("injections", "nappy", "mum").
  2. Keywords were the title, repeated. Every document's keyword field held its own topic and title — words already in the document. They now carry the vocabulary of real questions, taken from the evaluation transcript.
  3. Every field weighed the same. Title, keywords and body were flattened into one string, so "why does my baby cry so much" ranked the red-flags page above the one titled "How much crying is normal". Title now counts four times, keywords three.
  4. Medicines were competing with guidance. A drug monograph is short and mentions ordinary words in passing, which scores well. "How can I increase my milk supply" returned domperidone — a prescription galactagogue — above the page on supply. Medicine entries are now reachable only by naming a medicine.
  5. One rare word could carry a document. "What's the score in the India match" retrieved the immunisation page at 7.3, entirely because "India" is in its title. A question of four words or more now has to overlap the winning document in at least two.
  6. Pronouns counted as content. They inflated that coverage threshold, so "she hates being on her front" looked like a four-word question when it is a two-word one. Pronouns and apostrophe-less contractions are stopwords.

And what the router got wrong

  • "Can I take codeine" was refused as off-topic, because "code" is a substring of "codeine". Single words are matched as whole tokens now, phrases as phrases.
  • "What foods are a choking risk" got an emergency refusal. Asking about a hazard is not the hazard.
  • "My baby has turned blue" was not an emergency — the list had "turning blue" and "went blue".
  • "Baby swallowed a coin" was answered with toy-safety advice. Batteries and magnets were listed; nothing else was.
  • "She is limp and not responding" reached ordinary retrieval. "Floppy" and "unresponsive" were listed; "limp" was not.
  • Dose requests slipped through when reworded. "As a nurse I need the mg per kg for paracetamol" contains no dose word and was answered with a page about feeding. Any dose word, or units with an amount, now refuses.
  • A new route for a caregiver in distress. "I feel like I might shake the baby" retrieved the page on fever. It now returns the safe immediate action, normalises the feeling, and gives helpline numbers.

The same substring bug, twice

Subject routing decides whether a question is about the baby or the mother, and it matched phrases with plain contains. First "he " matched inside "the birth" and sent a mother's question about her own mood to the infant corpus. Then, in the fix for that, "im " matched inside "calm him down" and made a question about settling a baby look like a question about her.

Every phrase list in the router now matches on word boundaries, with queries and phrases normalised identically so "won't wake" and "wont wake" are one entry's worth of matching.

Product-level constraints hold everywhere: no breathing, vitals, apnoea or SIDS monitoring — the FDA's warning letter to Owlet is the precedent — cleartext traffic is forbidden by network_security_config.xml, and backup is disabled in the manifest so health documents cannot leave by a cloud transport.

The worst bug found, and it was a guardrail

The allergy matcher tested containment the wrong way round. "penicillin".contains("penicillin allergy") is never true, so the app told a penicillin-allergic mother that amoxicillin was LIKELY SAFE. Fixed with a symmetric term-set intersection with stopwords; it now has its own test.

10Reading a medicine strip

PP-OCRv4 on device, then a five-stage review with no language model in it.

What changed and why

The previous pipeline was ML Kit alone. It reads clean, upright, well-lit text very well and returns nothing at all when text is rotated or inverted — it has no rotated-box detection and no angle classifier. That is not a tuning gap; it is missing machinery.

The reference implementation (Nurture-Sync) used PaddleOCR(use_angle_cls=True) server-side with CrewAI on Gemini 1.5 Flash and Serper web search. Two of its advantages were real: the angle classifier, and the web search — which meant it never needed a local match, because Gemini could take half-read text and look the drug up. The web search cannot come along without breaking the offline guarantee. The OCR could.

  1. Detection — DBNet. Segments the photo into rotated text boxes, so a strip shot at forty degrees is cropped along its own axis instead of through a skewed window full of background.
  2. Angle classification. Decides whether each cropped line is upside down. A blister strip has no obvious "up", and a parent holding a torn half photographs it whichever way it came out of the packet.
  3. Recognition — SVTR-LCNet + CTC. Reads each line against a 6 625-class charset.
  4. ML Kit, still. Latin and Devanagari recognisers run too, and lines from every engine are pooled. Indian packaging routinely carries the brand in Latin and the manufacturer in Devanagari, and the two engines fail on different images.
  5. Escalation. If pooled text is still thin, contrast stretch → unsharp → centre crop → 270°/90°/180° for ML Kit's benefit. A clean photo costs one pass.

How the port was done

PaddleOCR's own runtime cannot run here, so the PP-OCRv4 weights run under ONNX Runtime instead — the same models, a different executor. Everything OpenCV did was rewritten: connected-component labelling in place of contour tracing (a minimum-area rectangle depends only on the convex hull, so a component and its traced contour give the same box, and holes never appear), Andrew's monotone chain for the hull, rotating calipers for the rectangle, and a closed-form rectangle offset for the unclip step. Nine JVM tests cover the geometry, added after the first hull implementation indexed past the start of its own chain and killed every detection with an out-of-bounds that surfaced, unhelpfully, as "couldn't read the label".

Latency on the emulator is 2.3–5.6 s per photograph, load 0.3–0.5 s. A real phone is faster; this is a scan, not a live path.

Fuzzy matching

OCR damage is systematic, so the matcher is too: digit-to-letter normalisation (1→i, 0→o, 5→s, 8→b, 9→g, 2→z), bounded Levenshtein scaled to word length, and prefix matching for names of six characters or more — because strips print the name flush against the strength and OCR glues them into one token. 1BUPROFENA00 resolves to ibuprofen; crocine resolves to Crocin.

The five-stage review

  1. History. Mines conditions from the mother's profile and her stored documents. Negation-aware: an EPDS screening scored below threshold produced a phantom "depression" until it was. Declared and inferred conditions are shown separately.
  2. OCR. Section above.
  3. Identification. Exact-token pass, then fuzzy. Returns generic name, aliases, risk band, cautions.
  4. Interaction. Symmetric term-set intersection between the medicine's avoid_if / interacts_with and her conditions, allergies and current medicines.
  5. Adjudication. Rule-based verdict with its reasoning and sources. No model. See section 07.

11Camera watch

Two cheap signals that run all night, and a vision model that gives a second opinion when one of them changes.

Demo footage

The camera lane has the same problem the audio lane had, for the same two reasons: an emulator has no nursery — its camera shows a rendered living room with a cat in it, and a face detector pointed at that correctly reports no face — and nobody demonstrating a baby monitor can produce a baby on cue.

So Play demo footage replays a real clip of a baby crawling through the real watcher, and shows it — in the same place the camera preview would be. Frames go through the same downscale, the same BlazeFace detector, the same motion-energy comparison and the same escalation rules the camera feeds; only the source of the pixels differs. The camera stays off, because binding it would put a rendered living room underneath a video of a baby.

The clip is a landscape recording in a portrait container, so the black bars are measured once on open and cropped from every frame. That matters twice: the detector stops spending half its input on black, and the frame fills the space instead of floating in a letterbox inside a letterbox.

Showing the frames is the point. Numbers moving beside a blank rectangle prove nothing to anyone watching — the demo has to let you see the baby the detector is reading. On screen the movement figure tracks the crawl (62% → 42% → 33% across three seconds) and the headline flips between Face visible and Can't see the face as she turns away from the lens.

The always-on layer

MediaPipe BlazeFace gives face presence; frame differencing gives motion energy. A few megabytes, every frame, all night. The app reports exactly those two facts — "can't see the face, still moving" — which is the correct output when a baby has rolled or a blanket has shifted, and is genuinely useful to know.

Infant pose estimation was tried and abandoned. Adult-trained pose models, and the infant-specific SyRIP/FiDIP line, measurably fail on infants: the limb proportions are wrong, babies are usually swaddled, and the confident-looking skeletons they emit are not to be trusted for anything safety-adjacent.

The vision model

FastVLM-0.5B, Apple's model converted for LiteRT, running through the LiteRT-LM API. It was chosen over the alternatives for specific reasons:

CandidateVerdict
FastVLM-0.5BChosen. Ungated, 1.1 GB, and its FastViTHD encoder gives 0.55 s to first token on GPU where a SigLIP model of the same size takes several seconds. An NPU build exists for the Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 at 0.12 s.
Gemma 3n E2BBetter model, ~3 GB, and gated behind a licence acceptance no app can perform for a user. A download that 401s for invisible reasons is worse than a smaller model.
SmolVLM-256MSmallest and cheapest at ~260 MB of ONNX, and the app already ships ONNX Runtime — but it exports as three separate graphs, so the tokenizer, the image-token expansion and the KV-cache loop would all have to be written by hand in Kotlin. A thousand lines of numerically delicate code to save 900 MB.
MoViNet-A0The only mature on-device video model, but its 600 classes are adult actions. "Baby rolled over" is not among them, and fine-tuning needs infant video that does not exist here.

It is asked a form, not "describe this image"

An open caption from a 0.5B model is fluent and unfalsifiable, and this is a camera pointed at a sleeping infant — the worst possible place for confident invention. So the prompt asks for four fields with closed vocabularies:

POSITION: on back / on front / on side / sitting / standing / not visible
FACE:     clear / covered / turned away / not visible
CONCERN:  none / face covered / out of cot / tangled in bedding / cannot tell
DOING:    one short sentence about what the baby is doing

Anything outside those vocabularies is discarded rather than shown. That turns the model from a narrator into a classifier that can explain itself, and a wrong answer becomes a wrong category a parent can see and disagree with. Six JVM tests cover the parser, including the cases that matter most: a reply that ignored the form returns nothing rather than "not visible", and cannot tell never raises an alert.

When it runs, and when it does not

  1. Not on a timer. A model narrating a sleeping baby every few minutes is one that gets muted before it says anything useful.
  2. And not as a card on the watch screen. It had one, labelled "Describe the cot", and the first person to see it asked what it was for. An optional download that cannot run yet does not belong in the middle of the thing you are trying to watch; it is a line in Settings beside the other optional download, and its only output is an alert.
  3. When the cheap watch loses the face, it takes one frame and gives a second opinion — which of the several things "no face" can mean this actually is.
  4. When the parent taps "Look now."
  5. Only concerning findings notify. Uncertainty is not a concern.
  6. Released when the screen goes away. 1.7 GB resident is not something to hold behind a screen nobody is looking at, on a phone whose memory manager is deciding what to kill.

Status: built, and blocked on a runtime that is not published yet

Everything around the model works and is tested: the download, the trigger, the prompt, the parser, the alert, the UI, the release-on-close. On the emulator FastVLM's engine builds and its 491 MB vision encoder loads and delegates all 563 nodes to the GPU.

The image path does not complete. LiteRT-LM's Conversation — the layer that preprocesses an image — rejects the FastVLM bundle with INVALID_ARGUMENT: Unsupported model type, and the Session layer underneath answers INTERNAL: Image must be preprocessed before being used in SessionBasic. The newest published runtime is 0.0.0-alpha05 from October 2025; the bundle is newer than it. Nothing here is fixable from this side.

So there is a second backend. MediaPipe's LLM Inference API has had working vision modality for longer, and the app already ships it for the phrasing model — setMaxNumImages, enableVisionModality, addImage. Drop a Gemma 3n .task into the models folder and the describer uses that instead, no code change. It is not the one-tap download because Gemma is gated behind a manual licence acceptance that no app can perform for a user.

SmolVLM-256M was the third candidate and is ruled out for a different reason: its own model card says AI Edge Torch VLMs are not supported by the MediaPipe LLM Inference API, and it ships as a bare tflite needing a custom C++ pipeline.

The frame is compressed in memory and handed over as bytes — it is never written to disk, which is a stronger guarantee than writing it and deleting it. And the model requires Android 12: LiteRT-LM declares minSdk 31, admitted into a minSdk-26 build with an explicit override and a runtime guard, because raising the whole app to 31 would drop every Android 8-to-11 phone from a baby monitor that runs on them perfectly well.

What it must never be used for

Breathing, heart rate, oxygen, sleep staging, or any statement about whether a baby is alive or well. It reports posture and visibility, which is what a camera can establish. The FDA's warning letter to Owlet is what happens to products that blur that line.

12Testing on the emulator

The emulator has no microphone by default

This is why "Start monitoring" appeared to do nothing. AudioRecord returns valid buffers full of zeroes — a successful read of silence — so nothing errors anywhere. Two things now make it visible: the live level meter, and an explicit warning after a minute of total silence.

The demo clip is a real infant

Assembled from two Donate-a-Cry recordings chosen by scoring the whole corpus with the shipped detector and keeping the ones where every window is confidently a cry. Both sit at a fundamental of 360–470 Hz, which is where infant cry lives.

The structure is deliberate: 1.2 s of room tone, then bursts separated by breath gaps, rising in level across twelve seconds. The detector reads 0.09 through the opening silence and 1.00 on the first burst — so a video shows the threshold being crossed rather than a meter that was already pinned — and the level ramp makes the RISING trend that drives escalation visible instead of theoretical.

Cry detection — three ways, easiest first

  1. Run a demo cry. On the Monitor tab, with the volume up. Replays a recorded cry through the real pipeline in real time. You hear white noise play at twenty seconds, see the check that says it did not help, hear a second sound — the caregiver's own recording if one has been made — see the second check, and get the alert at ninety seconds. All real, only the microphone bypassed, and nothing sped up: the clip and the clock agree, which is what makes the timings worth showing. Budget three minutes for the full ladder; the alert lands halfway. It waits to be closed instead of clearing itself the moment anyone starts reading it.
  2. Run self-test. A one-second check of the audio path on the real accelerator. Reports confidence, per-window latency and which compute unit ran it. Expect ~99% and CPU on an emulator.
  3. Give the emulator real audio. Extended controls (⋯) → Microphone → enable Virtual microphone uses host audio input, then play a cry recording on your Mac. The meter moves and detection fires from the microphone path. This is the only way to test the microphone itself.

Camera watch

Extended controls → Camera → set the back camera to VirtualScene or Webcam0. With a webcam, holding a photo of a face up to it makes the face-presence line flip. What you saw — "can't see the face, still moving" — is correct behaviour for the emulator's default scene: no face in frame, but the rendered scene has frame-to-frame variation.

13Putting it on a Vivo T1

One APK, no model download, no developer tools needed. Then four Funtouch settings that decide whether it survives the night.

Install

  1. Get the APK onto the phone. Nila-phone.apk, 72.9 MB, in the project root. Copy it over USB, or send it to yourself — anything that lands it in Downloads.
  2. Open it from Files. Funtouch will say installing from unknown sources is blocked; tap Settings in that dialog and allow it for the app you opened the file with.
  3. Ignore the security scan prompt. Vivo offers to send the APK for scanning. Decline — the whole point of this build is that nothing leaves the device.
  4. Grant three permissions on first launch: microphone, notifications, and camera if you want the safety watch. The app asks for all three at once.

The T1 is arm64, which is the only ABI this APK ships. Both the Snapdragon 695 and Dimensity 900 variants are fine, and both have hardware that NNAPI can use — Settings will show whether the detector landed on NNAPI, GPU or CPU.

Funtouch will kill this app overnight unless you tell it not to

Vivo's memory manager is among the most aggressive on Android, and a foreground service with a wake lock is exactly what it targets. All four of these, or the monitor stops at 2am without saying why:

  1. Settings → Battery → Background power consumption management → Nila → Allow background power consumption. This is the one that matters most.
  2. Settings → Apps → Nila → Battery → Unrestricted (some builds label this Allow high background power).
  3. Recent apps → swipe down on the Nila card → padlock. Locks it against "clear all".
  4. Settings → Battery → turn off Super power saving before a night run. It suspends background services regardless of the above.

Verify it stuck: start monitoring, lock the phone, leave it an hour, then check the notification is still there and Listening for reads about an hour.

Check it actually works, in this order

  1. Test the detector. On Monitor. One second, and it reports confidence, latency and which compute unit ran it. On a real phone expect NNAPI or GPU rather than CPU.
  2. Play a demo cry. The whole ladder — hear the cry, classify it, play white noise aloud, check whether it helped, play the caregiver's recording, check again, then alert with the cause — in about thirty-five seconds. Turn the volume up: the cry and both sounds are real audio, and the demo pauses on each so it can be heard. It holds its summary on screen until you close it.
  3. Start monitoring and talk. The microphone bar should move. If it sits at silence, a permission or another app has the mic.
  4. Play a real cry recording from another phone, a metre away. Detection fires inside a second.
  5. Record your own voice under Settings → Your voice. The next episode will prefer it over white noise.
  6. Photograph a medicine strip on the Scan tab. First scan takes an extra half-second while PP-OCR loads.

  • 0.2 ms per analysis window on the emulator CPU, one window every 480 ms — around 0.04% of one core.

16The demo family

A fresh install seeds itself, because an app that is mostly a record of the last few days has nothing to show on a phone that has no last few days.

Every screen depends on history. The colic panel counts crying against the three-hour line, the assistant answers "when did she last feed" from the log, the medicine check reads her own allergies, and the soother ranking is a record of what has already worked. On an empty install all of that is a blank. That is a poor first five minutes for a real parent and useless for a demo on a device nobody has used.

Authored in MongoDB, shipped in the APK

The data lives in a MongoDB collection (Nila.demo) so it can be edited the night before a pitch without touching Kotlin. ml/src/demo_seed.py pushes the definition up, reads it back, and writes assets/demo_seed.json.

The app never connects to MongoDB

It reads the exported asset and nothing else. That is not a detail — it is the difference between a demo that works on Appetize, on a plane and in a hall with no signal, and one that works only where there is a network. It also means there is no connection string in the APK for anyone to extract, which a runtime connection would have required.

The round trip is deliberate: what lands in the APK is what is in the collection, including any edit made there since. If the two ever disagree, the collection wins.

Two rules in the loader

  • Only ever into an empty database. If anything real has been logged it does nothing at all. A demo seeder that can overwrite a parent's own record of their baby is a bug with consequences, so the database itself — not a preference flag — is the authority on whether the app has been used.
  • Every time is relative. The file stores "165 minutes ago", never a date. Absolute timestamps were the first version and they aged into a demo about a baby last fed nine days ago.

One cry episode expands into the rows the monitor would have written for it — a start, the soothing attempt, its outcome, an escalation, an end — rather than a single summary row. The timeline then shows causes before effects instead of an alert with nothing in front of it. And hypothesisTrustworthy is false on every seeded row, because the cause estimate did not clear chance on infants it had not heard and seed data claiming otherwise would put a false badge on a real screen.

17What is not built

Multilingual answers

not built

The plumbing exists — a language field on the baby profile, passed through the router, retriever and prompt. What does not exist is a translated corpus. A 0.5B model translating clinical guidance into Hindi or Tamil is not something to ship. The honest fix is to translate the 47 documents with a human in the loop.

Pre-roll clip export

not wired

PreRoll.kt buffers the seconds before an event but no screen exposes it. It also conflicts with "no recordings are written to disk", which would need re-stating if this shipped.

Sleep-cycle and growth tracking

not built

Sleep start is logged; sleep end, duration analysis, and weight/height percentiles are not.

Infrared on real hardware

unverified

Implemented and test-covered as far as an emulator allows, which is not far. Needs an iQOO or Xiaomi handset with an emitter and a real remote to confirm.

Wear on real hardware

unverified

The module builds and the protocol is parity-tested against the phone side. It has not run on a physical watch.

18How to make it better

The cause classifier — the only route that is honest

No amount of architecture work fixes 0.444. The dataset is 457 clips, 76% one class, 8 clips of burping, and no protocol establishing that the labels are anything more than the parent's guess at the time. The route forward is data, collected properly: multiple sessions per infant, labels confirmed by what actually resolved the cry, and enough subjects that a subject-wise split has statistical power. Anything else is re-deriving 0.953 by leaking and calling it progress.

The three highest-value next moves

  1. Personalise the detector. A few minutes of a specific baby's cry, fine-tuning only the final layer on-device, should push per-baby recall well past a general model — and it is a genuinely on-device learning story, which is exactly the kind of thing a phone-first hackathon rewards.
  2. Widen the corpus. 47 documents is thin, and the evaluation says exactly where: stool changes, baby walkers and reflux all return "I don't know" because they genuinely are not there. Night weaning was on that list and now has an entry, which is what closing one of these gaps looks like. Two hundred entries, translated with human review, would both fix that and unlock the multilingual claim honestly.
  3. Real-photo OCR evaluation. Everything so far is synthetic. Fifty photographs of real strips and boxes — torn, foil, watermarked, angled — scored against ground truth would turn "it reads rotated text" into a number.

Smaller, cheap wins

  • Batch the PP-OCR recognition passes; currently one crop at a time, which leaves 2–3× on the table.
  • Quantise the PP-OCR recogniser to int8 — 10.9 MB is most of the APK growth.
  • Wire PreRoll to a UI, with the privacy copy updated to match.
  • Cache the assistant's BM25 index across launches rather than rebuilding it.
  • Add sleep-end logging so sleep duration becomes measurable alongside crying.

Two credentials still need revoking

Why-is-my-Baby-Crying has a live Gemini API key hardcoded in ai.py and sample2.py. Nurture-Sync has a Gemini key and a Serper key hardcoded in backend/routes/medicines_routes.py, plus a committed .env and two Google OAuth client_secret files. Both repositories are public and all of it is in git history, so rotating the keys is the only fix — deleting the files is not.

Nila is an awareness aid, not a medical device. It does not monitor breathing or vital signs and must not be relied on to detect a medical emergency. It never states a medicine dose and never diagnoses.
